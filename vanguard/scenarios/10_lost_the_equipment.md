Title: The missing guidon
Competency: Accountability
Situation:
You were in charge of putting the company's equipment away after an event. Rushing to catch your ride, you left the guidon leaning against the bleachers. The next morning it's gone. Your instructor asks the whole class if anyone knows what happened. Nobody has connected it to you yet.
Choice: Stay quiet — nobody knows it was your job.
Outcome: The instructor holds the whole company after class to look for it. Another Cadet gets blamed because they were the last one seen near the bleachers. You feel sick about it all week.
Strengths: You avoided an uncomfortable moment.
Weaknesses: Someone else paid for your mistake. If it comes out later, people will trust you less for hiding it than for losing it.
Choice: Admit it was you, but explain it wasn't really your fault because your ride was waiting.
Outcome: The instructor appreciates that you spoke up but points out that the ride doesn't change who was responsible. The class is still frustrated.
Strengths: You told the truth and cleared the other Cadet.
Weaknesses: Excuses weaken an apology. People hear "it wasn't my fault" louder than "I'm sorry."
Choice: Tell your instructor it was your responsibility, you left it, and offer to help find or replace it.
Outcome: The instructor thanks you for owning it. You and two friends check the lost-and-found and the custodian's office — it turns up there. You make a checklist for putting equipment away.
Strengths: You owned the mistake completely, fixed what you could, and kept it from happening again.
Weaknesses: It's embarrassing in the moment, and you may face a consequence.
Principles:
- Accountability means answering for what you do or fail to do.
- Integrity: do what's right, legally and morally — even when nobody is watching.
- Leaders who own their mistakes earn more trust than leaders who never seem to make any.
Other approaches:
Speak to your instructor privately right after class if saying it in front of everyone feels too hard — what matters is that you say it, and soon.
Reflection: Why is it harder to admit a mistake when nobody knows yet? What does owning it say about you?
Notice: Nobody knows it was your job — which is exactly when integrity matters.
Discussion:
- Why does adding 'but it wasn't really my fault' weaken an apology?
- Why do leaders who own their mistakes earn more trust?
- What would you offer to do to make it right?
Follow-up: Write a three-sentence 'I own it' statement for a small real mistake: what happened, that it was yours, and what you'll do now.
