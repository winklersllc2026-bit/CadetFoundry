Title: Nobody tells you anything
Competency: Being approachable
Situation:
You're the platoon sergeant for your Cadet platoon. Twice this month you found out about problems at the last minute — a Cadet who couldn't make the competition, and a squad that hadn't finished its part of the project. Last time a squad leader told you bad news, you snapped at them in front of everyone.
Choice: Tell your squad leaders they need to report problems sooner or there will be consequences.
Outcome: For a week, reports come in — but they're vague and everything is "fine." Then another problem surprises you at the last minute.
Strengths: You made the expectation clear.
Weaknesses: People who are afraid of your reaction hide bad news. Threats don't fix the reason they stopped telling you.
Choice: Fix the problems yourself as they come up and stop relying on your squad leaders.
Outcome: You're exhausted, and your squad leaders stop trying because you take over anyway.
Strengths: Things get done in the short term.
Weaknesses: You can't see everything yourself, and your team stops growing as leaders.
Choice: Apologize to the squad leader you snapped at, and tell your squad leaders you want to hear problems early — and that you'll stay calm when you do.
Outcome: It feels awkward. A few days later a squad leader tells you, early, that their squad is behind. You stay calm and help them make a plan. They meet the deadline.
Strengths: You fixed the real cause. Your team trusts you with bad news, so you can solve problems while they're still small.
Weaknesses: It takes humility, and you have to keep your promise every time.
Principles:
- Approachable leaders stay objective when receiving bad news and encourage open, candid communication.
- Climate is how people think and feel about the team — and the leader shapes it.
- Humility is part of a leader's character.
Other approaches:
Set a quick weekly check-in with each squad leader so problems have a regular place to come up before they become emergencies.
Reflection: How do you react when someone brings you bad news? What would make people more willing to tell you the truth?
Notice: The squad leaders stopped bringing bad news because of how the last piece of bad news was received.
Discussion:
- How does a leader's reaction to bad news change what they hear next time?
- Why is apologizing to the squad leader part of fixing the problem?
- What could a platoon sergeant do to make reporting problems early feel safe?
Follow-up: Cadets practice receiving bad news: a partner reports a made-up problem, and the leader responds calmly with a question and a next step.
