Title: The answers going around
Competency: Integrity
Situation:
A friend in your platoon texts the group chat a photo of next week's JROTC test answers. A few Cadets reply with thumbs-up. Your friend messages you directly: "You're the squad leader — you're not going to be weird about this, right?"
Choice: Say nothing and don't look at the answers.
Outcome: You stay honest yourself, but the answers keep spreading. When the instructor finds out, your squad is involved and people ask why the squad leader didn't do anything.
Strengths: You protected your own integrity and didn't use the answers.
Weaknesses: A leader's silence can look like approval. Others in your squad followed the crowd.
Choice: Tell your friend privately you're not okay with it, ask them to delete it, and tell the group you're not using it.
Outcome: Your friend is annoyed and a couple of Cadets call you uptight. Some delete it. You take the test without it and feel solid about your score.
Strengths: You stood up for what's right without making a public scene, and you gave others an easy way to do the right thing.
Weaknesses: It costs you socially for a while. The answers may still be out there.
Choice: Tell your instructor what was shared.
Outcome: The instructor changes the test. Some Cadets are upset, but the class takes a fair test. Your friend is angry at first, then admits it got out of hand.
Strengths: You protected fairness for everyone and used the right adult.
Weaknesses: It's hard, and it can strain a friendship. It works best if you've first told your friend where you stand.
Principles:
- Integrity: do what's right, legally and morally (one of the Army Values).
- Personal courage includes moral courage — standing up when the crowd goes the other way.
- Leaders set the standard by what they accept.
Other approaches:
Tell your friend first that you won't be part of it and why. If the answers keep spreading, tell your instructor. Doing both is often the strongest path.
Reflection: What would you do differently next time? What makes this choice hard for you?
Notice: The pressure comes from a friend and from the group, and the squad leader's silence would also send a message.
Discussion:
- Is staying silent and not looking at the answers enough for a squad leader? Why or why not?
- What makes moral courage harder than physical courage?
- What does a leader teach the squad by what they accept?
Follow-up: Cadets write the private message they would send their friend, keeping it respectful and clear.
