Title: Two Cadets arguing
Competency: Conflict resolution
Situation:
You're the squad leader. Your squad has 20 minutes to set up the color guard equipment for tonight's game. Two Cadets start arguing about who was supposed to bring the flag harnesses. Their voices are getting louder, the rest of the squad has stopped working to watch, and the clock is running.
Choice: Tell them both to stop arguing and get back to work — you'll sort it out later.
Outcome: The arguing stops and the squad gets moving. But neither Cadet feels heard, the harness question is still unanswered, and the tension shows up again before the game.
Strengths: You protected the deadline and showed you're in charge. Quick action kept the rest of the squad from standing around.
Weaknesses: "Later" often never comes. The real problem (the missing harnesses) wasn't solved, and the two Cadets may feel dismissed.
Choice: Pull the two Cadets aside, give each one a minute to explain, and decide together how to get the harnesses.
Outcome: It costs about three minutes. You learn the harnesses are in the supply closet, send one Cadet to get them, and both feel the problem was handled fairly. The squad finishes on time.
Strengths: You listened before deciding, moved the conflict away from the audience, and solved the actual problem.
Weaknesses: It takes time you may not always have. If you'd let the talk run long, the setup could have slipped.
Choice: Assign someone else to get the harnesses and let the two Cadets work it out on their own.
Outcome: The task gets done, but the two Cadets keep sniping at each other all evening. Others in the squad start taking sides.
Strengths: You kept the mission moving and didn't get pulled into the drama.
Weaknesses: A leader who ignores conflict lets it grow. The squad noticed you stepped around it.
Principles:
- Separate the people from the audience before you deal with a conflict.
- Listen to both sides before deciding — fast and fair are not opposites.
- Fix the problem, not just the noise.
- Respect: treat people as they should be treated (one of the Army Values).
Other approaches:
Give a clear, calm instruction to the whole squad first ("Everyone keep setting up — you two, with me"), then deal with the two Cadets. After the event, check in with both of them so the issue doesn't carry into next week.
Reflection: What would you do differently next time, and what would you say first?
