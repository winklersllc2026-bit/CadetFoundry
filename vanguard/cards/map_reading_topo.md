**"Topographic"** comes from two Greek words: **topo** ("place") and **graphos** ("drawn or written"). A topographic map shows a **three-dimensional surface on a flat piece of paper.**

**Contour lines** — sometimes called **"level lines"** — **join points of equal elevation**. Think of one as an imaginary line on the ground that stays at the same height the whole way. Most USGS maps draw contours in **brown**.

**Reading slope:** **the closer together the contour lines, the steeper the slope.** Lines far apart mean gentle or flat ground.

**Contour interval** — the change in elevation between one contour line and the next (for example, 10 feet). It depends mainly on the type of terrain and the map's scale.

**Index contours** — some contour lines are **thicker** and are **labeled with their elevation**, so you can read heights quickly.

**Scale** — the relationship between **distance on the map and distance on the ground**. On a **1:24,000** map, **1 inch = 2,000 feet**, showing an area in detail.

**Symbols** — maps use symbols for features like churches, bridges, streams and cliffs. On a real topographic map, **streams are blue** and contour lines are brown.

**Uses:** planning the best route for a hike, choosing a location for a school or an airport — and land navigation in JROTC.
