Title: The resume
Competency: Honesty
Situation:
You're applying for a summer job as a camp counselor. The posting asks for "leadership experience and first aid knowledge." A friend looks at your resume and says, "Just put that you were Cadet battalion commander and have CPR certification. Nobody checks." You were actually a squad leader for one semester, and you took a first aid class in JROTC but never got certified.
Choice: Take your friend's advice — everyone exaggerates a little.
Outcome: You get the interview. The director asks about your experience as battalion commander and when your CPR card expires. You stumble, and the director calls your JROTC instructor, who is one of your references. You don't get the job, and your instructor is disappointed.
Strengths: You were trying to compete for a job you wanted.
Weaknesses: Employers check, and references talk. Lying on a resume can lose you the job, your reference, and your reputation — even after you're hired.
Choice: Leave leadership and first aid off completely, since you don't think yours counts.
Outcome: Your resume looks thin and you don't get called back.
Strengths: Everything on it is true.
Weaknesses: You undersold real experience. Being a squad leader and taking a first aid class both count.
Choice: List what's true — squad leader, the first aid class, your service hours — and sign up for a CPR certification class before the job starts.
Outcome: In the interview you describe a time you led your squad through a tough inspection. The director likes your honesty and your plan to get certified. You get the job, on the condition you finish the CPR class.
Strengths: You told the truth, showed your real strengths with a story, and closed the gap you had.
Weaknesses: It takes more work, and some jobs may still go to someone with more experience.
Principles:
- Integrity: do what's right, legally and morally.
- A resume's top priority is showing you meet the job's qualifications — truthfully. If you lack a skill, plan how you'll show you can learn it.
- References carry weight with employers. Protect the trust of the people who vouch for you.
Other approaches:
Ask your instructor or a counselor to review your resume. They can help you describe real experience in strong, honest words.
Reflection: What real experiences do you have that you might be underselling? What would you need to learn to qualify for a job you want?
Notice: Leaving true experience off a resume is a problem too; the goal is accurate, not modest or inflated.
Discussion:
- Why is 'nobody checks' a weak reason to exaggerate?
- How can you describe real experience so it shows what you can do?
- If you're missing a skill a job wants, what can you say instead?
Follow-up: Cadets write three true resume lines about their own JROTC experience: a role, an action and a result.
