Title: The teammate who stopped participating
Competency: Teamwork
Situation:
Your team is preparing a group presentation for class. Jordan used to be one of the most involved Cadets, but for the last two weeks Jordan has been quiet, hasn't done their part, and sits apart from the group. The presentation is due Friday and the others are starting to complain about Jordan.
Choice: Tell the team you'll cover Jordan's part yourself so the presentation gets done.
Outcome: The presentation gets done, but you're exhausted and Jordan feels even more left out. The team learns that if someone stops working, the leader quietly carries it.
Strengths: You took responsibility for the mission and didn't blame anyone in public.
Weaknesses: You didn't find out why Jordan changed. Doing everything yourself doesn't scale and doesn't help Jordan.
Choice: Talk with Jordan privately: say what you've noticed, ask if everything's okay, and ask what part they can take on.
Outcome: Jordan says things have been hard at home and they've been embarrassed to say so. Together you pick a smaller piece Jordan can finish. You also mention a school counselor is someone Jordan can talk to. Jordan finishes their part.
Strengths: You noticed a change, approached it privately and without judgment, and found a way for Jordan to contribute.
Weaknesses: It takes courage and time. If Jordan didn't want to talk, you'd need patience and a fallback plan.
Choice: Report Jordan to the instructor for not doing the work.
Outcome: The instructor talks to Jordan, but Jordan feels the team went over their head without asking first. Trust inside the team drops.
Strengths: You were honest with your instructor, and adults are now aware.
Weaknesses: Skipping the conversation with your teammate first can feel like betrayal. Use the chain of command, but try to lead first.
Principles:
- A sudden change in behavior is information. Ask before you judge.
- Praise in public, correct in private.
- Care for your people: knowing them is part of leading them.
- If someone may be struggling seriously, involve a trusted adult — that's leadership, not tattling.
Other approaches:
Check in with Jordan privately first. If it's more than you can help with, encourage Jordan to talk to a counselor or the instructor — and tell your instructor yourself if you're worried about Jordan's safety. Meanwhile, rebalance the work so the team isn't punished for it.
Reflection: What would you do differently next time? How would you start the conversation with Jordan?
Notice: Jordan's change in behavior is the most important fact in the situation; the presentation is the second.
Discussion:
- What could be behind a sudden change like Jordan's?
- Why does it matter whether the conversation is private or in front of the team?
- When should a Cadet leader bring in a trusted adult, and how is that different from 'reporting' someone?
Follow-up: Each Cadet writes the first two sentences they would say to Jordan, then shares one with a partner.
