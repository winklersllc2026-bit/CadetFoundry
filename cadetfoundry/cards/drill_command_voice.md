A correctly given command is understood by everyone and has the **tone, cadence and snap** that bring a willing, correct and immediate response (para 3-13).

**Voice control** (paras 3-14 to 3-16)
- Match your loudness to the size of the unit. Stand **front and center**, facing the unit.
- Don't strain: tightening your neck muscles causes hoarseness and jumbled commands. Power comes from **good posture, proper breathing and confidence**.
- The best posture for giving commands is the **Position of Attention** — the formation copies its leader's posture.
- Breathe from the **diaphragm**, the large muscle below your chest.

**Distinctiveness** (paras 3-17, 3-18) — use your lips, tongue and lower jaw to say every syllable clearly. Practice slowly, stretching the syllables, then speed up.

**Inflection** — the rise and fall of your voice (paras 3-19 to 3-22):
- **Preparatory command** (*Forward*): a **rising** inflection. Start near your **natural speaking pitch** — a common beginner mistake is starting too high.
- **Command of execution** (**MARCH**): sharper and slightly higher, **with snap**.
- **Combined commands** (**FALL IN**): no inflection — high, uniform and loud.

**Cadence in commands** (paras 3-23, 3-24) — a steady, rhythmic flow. When marching, allow **one step (count)** between the preparatory command and the command of execution.

The best way to develop a command voice is to **practice** (para 3-21).
